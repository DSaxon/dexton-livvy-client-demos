Mrs. Miller's Legacy

Her Table Was Only Part of Her Legacy

The woman whose recipe inspired YAM JAM also helped change the course of educational history in Washington, D.C.

During segregation, Black Deaf children living in Washington, D.C. were denied admission to the Kendall School for the Deaf on the campus of Gallaudet University. While white Deaf children could attend school in the District, Black Deaf children were required to travel to Baltimore, Maryland, to receive an education.

Mrs. Louise B. Miller and her husband, Luther, were raising three Deaf sons and a hearing daughter. Unwilling to accept that her children could not receive the same educational opportunities available to white children in their own city, Mrs. Miller took legal action.

In 1952, she became the lead plaintiff in Miller v. Board of Education of the District of Columbia, a federal lawsuit brought on behalf of her son Kenneth and other Black Deaf children who had been excluded from Kendall School.

On July 3, 1952, the United States District Court for the District of Columbia ruled in favor of the children, establishing that the District could not require Black Deaf students to leave Washington to obtain an education that was available locally to white Deaf students.

The decision led to the establishment of Kendall School Division II on Gallaudet's campus in 1953, allowing Black Deaf children to attend school in Washington rather than being sent to Maryland.

Mrs. Miller's legal victory came two years before the United States Supreme Court's landmark decision in Brown v. Board of Education in 1954. Her case became an important part of the legal history preceding the desegregation of American public schools.

What began with Mrs. Miller's determination to secure an education for her children helped change educational opportunities for Black Deaf families throughout Washington, D.C.

A Legacy Preserved at Gallaudet University

Mrs. Miller's work is also being commemorated on the campus of Gallaudet University through the Louise B. Miller Pathways and Gardens: A Legacy to Black Deaf Children project.

The project honors Mrs. Miller, the students of Kendall School Division II and an important period in the history of education for Black Deaf children in Washington, D.C.

The Woman Her Family Remembers

Washington has honored Mrs. Miller for her advocacy, and her family continues to honor the many other dimensions of her life.

She was a mother, wife, seamstress, cook and family matriarch whose home was filled with the people she loved. The recipes she prepared and the traditions she established remained with her children and grandchildren, becoming part of the family's shared history.

YAM JAM preserves one of those traditions.

Louise B. Miller Way

In 2025, Washington, D.C. ceremonially renamed the 1200 block of T Street NW between 12th and 13th Streets Louise B. Miller Way, honoring her advocacy for Black Deaf children and the families whose lives were changed by her determination to challenge an unjust educational system.

The street designation recognizes Mrs. Miller's place in Washington history and ensures that future generations will encounter her name and learn about her contributions.
